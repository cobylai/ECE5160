Overview

This lab integrates two VL53L1X time-of-flight (ToF) distance sensors with the Artemis over I²C using QWIIC. The work focuses on reliable wiring, confirming I²C communication, resolving address conflicts for dual-sensor use, and evaluating measurement stability across static and dynamic tests.

1. Lab Tasks

1.1 Power and wiring

After confirming the wiring color scheme, the battery leads were soldered to a connector compatible with the Artemis board to provide a secure and reliable power interface. Care was taken to ensure proper polarity and mechanical strain relief to prevent accidental disconnection during operation. (insert 1.png)

1.2 QWIIC leads on the ToF boards

The supplied QWIIC cables were cut and soldered directly to the ToF sensor boards. These connections allow the sensors to plug into a QWIIC splitter, enabling multiple devices to share a single I²C port on the Artemis while maintaining a compact and organized wiring layout. (insert 2.png)

1.3 Confirm I²C communication

With the Artemis connected to a single ToF sensor, the Example05_wire_I2C scanner was executed. The scan detected a device at address 0x29, confirming that the sensor was powered, communicating properly, and using its factory default 7-bit I²C address.

1.4 Dual-sensor addressing with XSHUT

Because both ToF sensors share the same default address, they cannot operate simultaneously on the same bus without modification. To resolve this, a wire was soldered from Artemis pin 8 to the XSHUT pin of one sensor. This hardware shutdown line allows that sensor to be temporarily disabled during initialization, enabling the active sensor’s address to be reassigned before bringing the second device online. Once configured, both sensors can operate concurrently and be addressed independently. (insert 3.png and 4.png side by side)

    distanceSensor1.clearInterrupt();

2. Distance Modes

Time-of-flight sensors support short, medium, and long distance modes that trade range for speed and measurement stability. Short mode offers the fastest update rate and strongest signal return at close distances, making it well-suited for rapid collision avoidance, but its maximum range is limited. Medium mode provides a balanced compromise between speed and distance. Long mode extends the usable range substantially, which is advantageous for room-scale perception and mapping tasks, though it typically reduces update frequency and may introduce slightly higher noise. For this project, long mode was selected because it still provides sufficient accuracy at short distances while enabling the detection of distant obstacles that are more relevant for navigation.

4.2 Surface and lighting effects

Under controlled indoor lighting, smooth matte surfaces produced the most consistent readings because they return a strong, diffuse infrared signal back to the receiver. Raised or “fluffy” textures tend to scatter the emitted light into many directions, reducing the fraction that returns to the sensor and increasing jitter. Dark materials often absorb more infrared energy, lowering return strength and making measurements less stable, especially near the upper end of the sensor’s range. Highly reflective surfaces can also cause failures, since specular reflections can redirect the return away from the receiver or create multipath effects that lead to incorrect distances. Bright ambient infrared (for example direct sunlight) can further degrade stability by increasing background noise in the receiver.

5. Infrared Distance Sensor Types

Infrared distance sensing can be implemented using several distinct techniques, most notably optical triangulation sensors and time-of-flight modules. Triangulation devices project an infrared spot at a fixed angle and infer distance by measuring where the reflected spot lands on an internal detector. They typically output an analog voltage that varies nonlinearly with distance. These sensors are inexpensive and fast, but they have a limited useful range and can be sensitive to target angle, surface finish, and geometry. Time-of-flight sensors instead emit short infrared pulses and compute distance from the measured travel time using on-board timing circuits and digital processing. This approach supports longer ranges and often provides more repeatable measurements across different materials, which is useful for robotics tasks like obstacle avoidance and mapping. The trade-offs are higher cost, more configuration and software overhead, and usually a narrower field of view than simple triangulation sensors.

Discussion

The most important integration challenge in this lab was enabling two identical ToF sensors to share a single I²C bus. Using XSHUT to temporarily disable one sensor during startup made it possible to reassign an address and bring both devices online reliably. After configuration, the sensors produced stable readings with very small variation in both static and moving tests, indicating that the ToF pipeline is suitable for real-time ranging on the robot.
